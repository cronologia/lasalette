About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). The apparition is recorded here as a REPORTED event: the dataset states who reported what and when, and what the Church ruled and when, citing the ruling document — it never asserts the supernatural claim as fact. Contested claims are attributed to their authors; dates not yet verified against a primary source are flagged with a ? mark. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A single Marian apparition reported on 19 September 1846 on a mountain above La Salette-Fallavaux (French Alps, then diocese of Grenoble) by two shepherd children — recorded here as the children's account and as the Church judgments passed on it, not as an asserted fact.[1] [5]
The seers
Mélanie Calvat, aged 14 (the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia says 15), and Maximin Giraud, aged 11 — two shepherd children of Corps who reported a weeping "Beautiful Lady" and a message delivered partly in French, partly in the local Occitan dialect.[1] [5] [6]
Church approval status
Two separate judgments about two separate objects. (1) The apparition: declared to bear "all the characteristics of truth" and worthy of belief by Bishop Philibert de Bruillard of Grenoble on 19 September 1851. (2) Mélanie's later, expanded "Secret": restricted by the Holy Office — a decree of 21 December 1915 ordered the faithful not to discuss the secret under its various forms, and a decree of 9 May 1923 placed the 1922 reprint of her 1879 booklet on the Index of Forbidden Books. The 1915 decree states expressly that devotion to Our Lady of La Salette itself is not forbidden.[1] [3] [4]
Institutions born of it
The Sanctuary (later Basilica) of Our Lady of La Salette on the apparition site, and the Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette, both announced by Bishop de Bruillard's pastoral letter of 1 May 1852.[6] [8] [2]

1846 1846-09-19 La Salette-Fallavaux, France Two children report an apparition of a weeping "Beautiful Lady" — Mélanie Calvat (14) and Maximin Giraud (11), shepherd children from Corps, report that at about 3 p.m., on the mountain pasture above La Salette-Fallavaux, a weeping woman surrounded by light spoke to them — in French and then in the local Occitan dialect — delivering a public message centred on blasphemy, Sunday observance and conversion, and entrusting each child with a personal secret. All content of the event rests on the children's own accounts.[1] [5] [6]

Grenoble, France Bishop de Bruillard opens the canonical investigation — Bishop Philibert de Bruillard of Grenoble orders an investigation of the children's report and appoints a commission of clergy and theologians to examine it judicially; the commission concludes, per the Catholic Encyclopedia, that the reality of the apparition should be admitted. The exact dates of the commissions' appointment and sittings are not yet verified against a reachable source.[1] [5]
1850s
1851 1851-07 Grenoble, France The children's secrets are written down and sent to Pius IX — At the bishop's instance the two seers commit their personal secrets to paper — Maximin's letter is dated Grenoble, 3 July 1851, Mélanie's 6 July 1851 — and the sealed texts are carried to Rome by two envoys of Bishop de Bruillard and delivered to Pope Pius IX in July 1851. Accounts differ on the envoys' identity (canons Gerin and Rousselot, or vicars-general Melin and Rousselot). Neither text was published by the Holy See; Maximin's secret was never published at all.[9] [5] [6]
1851 1851-09-19 Grenoble, France Bishop de Bruillard declares the apparition worthy of belief — Five years to the day after the reported event, Bishop Philibert de Bruillard issues his doctrinal pronouncement: the apparition of the Blessed Virgin to the two shepherds on 19 September 1846 "bears within itself all the characteristics of truth", and the faithful are justified in believing it certain. This is the key diocesan approval document; the Catholic Encyclopedia dates the declaration 16 November 1851, a discrepancy recorded here rather than resolved.[1] [6] [5]
1852 1852-05-01 Grenoble, France Pastoral letter: a sanctuary decreed, the Missionaries of La Salette founded — Bishop de Bruillard publishes a pastoral letter announcing the construction of a sanctuary on the mountain of La Salette and creating a body of diocesan missionaries he names the Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette, charged with serving the pilgrims and spreading the message the children reported.[8] [6] [2]
1852 1852-05-25 La Salette-Fallavaux, France Cornerstone of the sanctuary church laid on the mountain — The first stone of the church is laid on the apparition site at about 1,800 m altitude, before a large assembly of the faithful; the building was completed in 1865.[5] [8]
1855 1855 ? Grenoble, France Bishop Ginoulhiac confirms his predecessor's judgment — Jacques Ginoulhiac, de Bruillard's successor as Bishop of Grenoble, confirms the 1851 decision, stating — per the sanctuary's account — that "the task of the shepherds is complete; that of the Church begins."[1]
1870s
1875 1875-03-01 Corps, France Death of Maximin Giraud — Maximin Giraud, who after unsettled years of study and odd employments had returned to his native Corps, dies there aged about 39. His personal secret was never published.[5] [6]
1879 1879 La Salette-Fallavaux, France The sanctuary church is consecrated and made a minor basilica — The church on the apparition site is officially consecrated and raised to the rank of minor basilica.[8]
1879 1879-11-15 Lecce, Italy Mélanie publishes her expanded secret at Lecce — Mélanie Calvat publishes at Lecce, as "L'apparition de la très Sainte Vierge sur la montagne de La Salette", a text of her secret far longer than the one sent to Pius IX in 1851, with the imprimatur of Salvatore Luigi Zola, Bishop of Lecce, who had received her in his diocese. The expanded text — including its much-quoted apocalyptic passages — is her own later redaction; its relation to what she wrote in 1851 is precisely what the subsequent controversy disputes.[7] [6] [5]
1880s
1880 1880-08 ? Rome, Italy The Holy Office asks that the Lecce booklet be withdrawn — After the Bishop of Troyes denounces the Lecce publication, Cardinal Prospero Caterini of the Holy Office writes — per the accounts cited — that the Holy Office is displeased with the book and wishes copies withdrawn from circulation. This is a disciplinary reaction to the expanded secret, not a revocation of the 1851 approval of the apparition.[7]
1900s
1904 1904-12-14 ? Altamura, Italy Death of Mélanie Calvat — Mélanie Calvat dies at Altamura in southern Italy, where she had settled after decades of wandering between convents and countries. Sources disagree on the day — 14 December (Wikipedia) or 15 December (Catholic Encyclopedia).[7] [5]
1910s
1915 1915-12-21 Rome, Italy Holy Office decree: the faithful ordered not to discuss the "Secret de la Salette" — The Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office issues a decree — "Decretum circa vulgo dictum «Secret de la Salette»" — commanding all the faithful, of every country, not to treat or discuss the so-called Secret of La Salette, its diverse forms, or its application to present or future times, under any pretext or form, in books, pamphlets or articles signed or anonymous, under pain of canonical sanctions. The decree states expressly that it does not forbid devotion to the Blessed Virgin invoked as Reconciler of La Salette. It disciplines discussion of the expanded secret; it does not touch the 1851 judgment on the apparition.[3]
1920s
1923 1923-05-09 Rome, Italy Mélanie's booklet is placed on the Index of Forbidden Books — The Holy Office proscribes and condemns the booklet "L'apparition de la très Sainte Vierge sur la sainte montagne de la Salette" — the 1922 Société Saint-Augustin reprint of the text published by Mélanie — ordering that copies be withdrawn from the hands of the faithful; Pope Pius XI approves the resolution. The condemnation names Mélanie's booklet, a separate object from the apparition approved in 1851.[4] [7]
2010s
2016 2016-03-18 Rome, Italy Our Lady of La Salette entered in the liturgical calendar for France — The Congregation for Divine Worship inscribes the celebration of Our Lady of La Salette in the proper liturgical calendar for France as an optional memorial on 19 September — a further act of the ordinary ecclesiastical recognition that has always concerned the apparition and the devotion, not the expanded secrets.[8]

Key figures

Mélanie Calvat

1831–1904 · France

The elder of the two seers; a shepherd girl of Corps, aged 14 at the reported apparition (15 per the Catholic Encyclopedia). Later a religious in and out of several convents in France, England and Italy; author of the expanding versions of her secret culminating in the 1879 Lecce booklet.[7] [5]

Maximin Giraud

c. 1835–1875 · France

The younger seer; a shepherd boy of Corps, aged 11 at the reported apparition. His later life was unsettled — studies and employments taken up and abandoned — and his personal secret was never published. Died at Corps.[5] [6]

Philibert de Bruillard

France

Bishop of Grenoble at the time of the reported apparition. Ordered the canonical investigation, issued the doctrinal pronouncement of 19 September 1851 declaring the apparition worthy of belief, and by the pastoral of 1 May 1852 decreed the sanctuary and founded the Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette.[1] [6] [2]

Jacques Ginoulhiac

France

De Bruillard's successor as Bishop of Grenoble; confirmed the 1851 judgment in 1855, per the sanctuary's account.[1]

Salvatore Luigi Zola

Italy

Bishop of Lecce; protector of Mélanie in her Italian years and grantor of the imprimatur for her 1879 booklet — the imprimatur of a local ordinary, not an approval by the Holy See.[7]

Related organizations

Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette

Founded 1852 · Grenoble, France

Congregation of priests founded by Bishop de Bruillard's pastoral of 1 May 1852 to serve the sanctuary's pilgrims and spread the message the children reported; per the congregation's own timeline it was canonically approved by Rome on 18 August 1879. It is the shrine-serving institution, not a party to the secrets controversy.[2] [6]

Sanctuary of Our Lady of La Salette

Founded 1852 · La Salette-Fallavaux, France

The shrine decreed by de Bruillard's 1852 pastoral on the apparition site; church begun 1852, completed 1865, consecrated and made a minor basilica in 1879. Its official history page is a preferred primary source for this dataset's institutional dates.[1] [8] [5]

Disambiguation & nuance

La Salette is contested terrain in a specific way: the same name covers an apparition approved by a bishop in 1851 and a body of later 'secret' texts restricted by the Holy Office in 1915 and 1923. Collapsing the two is the single most common error in the literature, sympathetic and critical alike.

The apparition ≠ the "Secret of La Salette"

The 1851 approval and the later disciplinary acts are separate judgments about separate objects. Bishop de Bruillard's 19 September 1851 pronouncement declared the apparition itself worthy of belief. The Holy Office's 1915 decree and the 1923 Index placement concern Mélanie's later, expanded secret texts — the 1915 decree even states expressly that devotion to Our Lady of La Salette is not forbidden. Neither act revokes the other.[3] [4] [1]

Reported, not asserted

Everything said about the 19 September 1846 event rests on the accounts of two children, examined and judged credible by a diocesan investigation. This dataset records who reported what and when, and what the Church ruled and when; it does not assert the supernatural claim as fact, and it does not deny it either.[5] [1]

The 1851 secret ≠ the 1879 text

What Mélanie wrote for Pius IX in July 1851 and what she published at Lecce in 1879 are not established to be the same text: the 1879 booklet is far longer, and her versions grew over the intervening decades. Which text is 'the' secret is exactly what the controversy disputes; this dataset carries the dispute, attributed and dated, without resolving it.[7] [9] [6]

La Salette-Fallavaux ≠ the sanctuary

La Salette-Fallavaux is the Alpine commune; the reported apparition site and the basilica stand on the mountain above it, at about 1,800 m. "La Salette" also names the two religious congregations born of the shrine (the Missionaries and the Sisters), which are institutions, not the event.[1] [8]
