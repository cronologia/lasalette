Our Lady of La Salette — Cronologia

Chronology of the reported apparition at La Salette in its setting — the famine year, the shrine, the congregation and the Church's judgments (1826–2016)

An open, source-referenced chronology of the Marian apparition reported at La Salette-Fallavaux in 1846: the famine year and the diocese it happened in, the cures reported afterwards, the diocesan approval of 1851, the basilica and the Missionaries of La Salette, and the later controversy over the "Secrets of La Salette".

How far the case went

Two things were judged here, by different authorities, about different objects, and this page never adds them up. The apparition reported on 19 September 1846 was investigated by the Bishop of Grenoble and declared worthy of belief on 19 September 1851; that judgment has never been revoked. The Holy Office acts of 1915 and 1923 concern a different object — the expanded "Secret" texts Mélanie Calvat wrote and published decades afterwards — and the 1915 decree states in so many words that devotion to Our Lady of La Salette is not thereby forbidden. No Roman doctrinal ruling on the 1846 apparition itself has been located, in either direction. Reading the 1915 and 1923 decrees as Rome's verdict on the apparition is the most common error in the literature, sympathetic and critical alike.

Step by step, with the documents

Parish level — local inquiry September–October 1846

·No record found that this step took place

The parish clergy of La Salette, in the diocese of Grenoble

No distinct parish-level canonical inquiry has been located. What the sources record at this level is pastoral rather than judicial: the children were urged to tell the parish priest of La Salette, who took notes and recounted their account in his sermon the next morning, and the English Wikipedia article states that on 16 October 1846 the parish priest wrote down where the two children's accounts differed. Searched for a parish-level process or finding in the sanctuary's own history, the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia article and the English Wikipedia article: all three pass straight from the children's report to the bishop's commissions, and none names a local canonical inquiry or a ruling by one. That is a statement about the record, not a claim that nothing happened at La Salette before 1847.

[6] [1] [5]

Diocese of Grenoble — the apparition 1847 – 19 September 1851

✓Investigated — concluded in favour

Bishop Philibert de Bruillard, with commissions of clergy and theologians

The bishop appointed a commission to examine the event judicially; the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia records that it concluded the reality of the apparition should be admitted. After what the sanctuary describes as five years of rigorous investigation, de Bruillard's doctrinal pronouncement of 19 September 1851 declared that the apparition of the Blessed Virgin to the two shepherds bears within itself all the characteristics of truth and that the faithful have good grounds to hold it certain, and authorised the cult. This is the judgment on the apparition, it is diocesan, and it has never been revoked. (The Catholic Encyclopedia instead dates the declaration 16 November 1851; the discrepancy is recorded here, not resolved.)

[1] [6] [5]

Rome — the 1846 apparition itself 1851 onwards

·No record found that this step took place

The Holy See

No Roman doctrinal ruling on the apparition has been located, in either direction. The children's sealed secrets were carried to Pius IX in July 1851 and no published papal judgment on them or on the apparition followed; the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia sums the position up as "As Rome uttered no decision the strife was prolonged between the disputants". The two Roman acts this chronology does cite, of 1915 and 1923, name Mélanie's secret texts and not the apparition; the 2016 act of the Congregation for Divine Worship inscribing the memorial of Our Lady of La Salette in the proper calendar for France concerns the liturgy and the devotion, not the truth of the 1846 event. Approval of this apparition rests where it was given in 1851: with the diocese.

[5] [8]

Holy Office — Mélanie's "Secret de la Salette" (not the apparition) 21 December 1915

✗Investigated — concluded against

Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office

The decree "Decretum circa vulgo dictum «Secret de la Salette»" (AAS 7 (1915), pp. 594–595) commands the faithful of every country not to treat or discuss the so-called Secret of La Salette in its various forms, or its application to present or future times, in books, pamphlets or articles, signed or anonymous, under pain of canonical sanctions. Its object is the secret texts. The same decree states expressly that it does not forbid devotion to the Blessed Virgin invoked as Reconciler of La Salette, and it leaves the diocesan judgment of 1851 on the apparition untouched.

[3]

Holy Office — Mélanie's printed booklet (not the apparition) 9 May 1923

✗Investigated — concluded against

Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office; approved by Pius XI

The Holy Office proscribed and condemned the booklet "L'apparition de la très Sainte Vierge sur la sainte montagne de la Salette" — the 1922 Société Saint-Augustin reprint of the text Mélanie published at Lecce in 1879 — ordering copies withdrawn from the hands of the faithful; Pius XI approved the resolution the same day (AAS 15 (1923), pp. 287–288). The condemned object is a printed book by one of the seers, not the apparition of 1846 and not the diocesan judgment of 1851.

[4] [7]

About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). The apparition is recorded here as a REPORTED event: the dataset states who reported what and when, and what the Church ruled and when, citing the ruling document — it never asserts the supernatural claim as fact. Events before 1846 (the subsistence crisis, the diocese of Grenoble under de Bruillard) are CONTEXT for the setting, offered neither as explanation of the report nor as corroboration of it. Reported cures are handled the same way: the datable thing is the ACCOUNT — that a cure was reported, by whom, when it began to circulate, and when the commission or Rousselot's printed report took it up. No cure is recorded as having happened; La Salette has no medical bureau and no cure connected with it carries an act of recognition. Attendance figures are attributed to whoever publishes them and are left unreconciled where they disagree. The two Church judgments this dataset carries are about two different objects — the apparition (approved 1851) and the expanded "secrets" (restricted 1915 and 1923) — and are never merged. Contested claims are attributed to their authors; dates not yet verified against a primary source are flagged with a ? mark. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A single Marian apparition reported on 19 September 1846 on a mountain above La Salette-Fallavaux (French Alps, then diocese of Grenoble) by two shepherd children — recorded here as the children's account and as the Church judgments passed on it, not as an asserted fact.[1] [5]
The seers
Mélanie Calvat, aged 14 (the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia says 15), and Maximin Giraud, aged 11 — two shepherd children of Corps who reported a weeping "Beautiful Lady" and a message delivered partly in French, partly in the local Occitan dialect.[1] [5] [6]
Church approval status
Two separate judgments about two separate objects. (1) The apparition: declared to bear "all the characteristics of truth" and worthy of belief by Bishop Philibert de Bruillard of Grenoble on 19 September 1851. (2) Mélanie's later, expanded "Secret": restricted by the Holy Office — a decree of 21 December 1915 ordered the faithful not to discuss the secret under its various forms, and a decree of 9 May 1923 placed the 1922 reprint of her 1879 booklet on the Index of Forbidden Books. The 1915 decree states expressly that devotion to Our Lady of La Salette itself is not forbidden.[1] [3] [4]
Institutions born of it
The Sanctuary (later Basilica) of Our Lady of La Salette on the apparition site, and the Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette, both announced by Bishop de Bruillard's pastoral letter of 1 May 1852.[6] [8] [2]
Reported cures
Cures were claimed at the mountain from the autumn of 1846, and the reports — with the press controversy around them — are what brought Bishop de Bruillard to open the canonical inquiry in July 1847; the commission examined them, and Canon Rousselot printed the case for them in his report to the bishop. That is as far as this dataset goes: it records who reported what and when, and never that a cure occurred. La Salette has no medical bureau and no equivalent of the Lourdes recognition acts — the French Wikipedia article states plainly that the Church made no scientific inquiry into these "declarations of miracles" — so no cure connected with the shrine carries a dated act of recognition, and none is recorded here as a fact.[13] [12] [5]
Pilgrimage today
Two figures circulate and they do not agree. Villes Sanctuaires en France, the association of French shrine towns of which La Salette is a member, gives nearly 200,000 pilgrims and visitors a year; the French Wikipedia article gives about 300,000. Neither states a method, and no audited count published by the sanctuary itself was reachable. Both numbers are attributed to whoever publishes them and the gap is left standing.[15] [14]

1846 1846 France and continental Europe The subsistence crisis of the Hungry Forties reaches its worst year — Potato blight arrived on the continent in the summer of 1845 and spread within weeks; the cereal harvests failed as well, and food prices reached their peak in the first half of 1847. France recorded roughly ten thousand famine-related deaths — far fewer than Ireland, and still a subsistence crisis of a kind Western Europe would not see again. Key figures

Mélanie Calvat

1831–1904 · France

The elder of the two seers; a shepherd girl of Corps, aged 14 at the reported apparition (15 per the Catholic Encyclopedia). Later a religious in and out of several convents in France, England and Italy; author of the expanding versions of her secret culminating in the 1879 Lecce booklet.[7] [5]

Maximin Giraud

c. 1835–1875 · France

The younger seer; a shepherd boy of Corps, aged 11 at the reported apparition. His later life was unsettled — studies and employments taken up and abandoned — and his personal secret was never published. Died at Corps.[5] [6]

Philibert de Bruillard

France

Bishop of Grenoble at the time of the reported apparition. Ordered the canonical investigation, issued the doctrinal pronouncement of 19 September 1851 declaring the apparition worthy of belief, and by the pastoral of 1 May 1852 decreed the sanctuary and founded the Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette.[1] [6] [2]

Jacques Ginoulhiac

France

De Bruillard's successor as Bishop of Grenoble; confirmed the 1851 judgment in 1855, per the sanctuary's account.[1]

Salvatore Luigi Zola

Italy

Bishop of Lecce; protector of Mélanie in her Italian years and grantor of the imprimatur for her 1879 booklet — the imprimatur of a local ordinary, not an approval by the Holy See.[7]

Related organizations

Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette

Founded 1852 · Grenoble, France

Congregation of priests founded by Bishop de Bruillard's pastoral of 1 May 1852 to serve the sanctuary's pilgrims and spread the message the children reported; per the congregation's own timeline it was canonically approved by Rome on 18 August 1879. It is the shrine-serving institution, not a party to the secrets controversy.[2] [6]

Sanctuary of Our Lady of La Salette

Founded 1852 · La Salette-Fallavaux, France

The shrine decreed by de Bruillard's 1852 pastoral on the apparition site; church begun 1852, completed 1865, consecrated and made a minor basilica in 1879. Its official history page is a preferred primary source for this dataset's institutional dates.[1] [8] [5]

Disambiguation & nuance

La Salette is contested terrain in a specific way: the same name covers an apparition approved by a bishop in 1851 and a body of later 'secret' texts restricted by the Holy Office in 1915 and 1923. Collapsing the two is the single most common error in the literature, sympathetic and critical alike.

The apparition ≠ the "Secret of La Salette"

The 1851 approval and the later disciplinary acts are separate judgments about separate objects. Bishop de Bruillard's 19 September 1851 pronouncement declared the apparition itself worthy of belief. The Holy Office's 1915 decree and the 1923 Index placement concern Mélanie's later, expanded secret texts — the 1915 decree even states expressly that devotion to Our Lady of La Salette is not forbidden. Neither act revokes the other.[3] [4] [1]

Reported, not asserted

Everything said about the 19 September 1846 event rests on the accounts of two children, examined and judged credible by a diocesan investigation. This dataset records who reported what and when, and what the Church ruled and when; it does not assert the supernatural claim as fact, and it does not deny it either.[5] [1]

The 1851 secret ≠ the 1879 text

What Mélanie wrote for Pius IX in July 1851 and what she published at Lecce in 1879 are not established to be the same text: the 1879 booklet is far longer, and her versions grew over the intervening decades. Which text is 'the' secret is exactly what the controversy disputes; this dataset carries the dispute, attributed and dated, without resolving it.[7] [9] [6]

La Salette-Fallavaux ≠ the sanctuary

La Salette-Fallavaux is the Alpine commune; the reported apparition site and the basilica stand on the mountain above it, at about 1,800 m. "La Salette" also names the two religious congregations born of the shrine (the Missionaries and the Sisters), which are institutions, not the event.[1] [8]

A reported cure ≠ a recognized miracle

The cures claimed at La Salette are part of this record in exactly one way: as REPORTS. The event dated is the reporting — who claimed a cure, when the claim began to circulate, and when the commission or Canon Rousselot's printed report took it up. No cure is recorded as having happened, and no beneficiary is named in a title. Nor is there a recognition act to record: unlike Lourdes, La Salette has no medical bureau and no bishop's declaration that a named cure is miraculous. The cures were part of the evidence the diocesan judgment of 1851 weighed; they are not themselves approved miracles, and the 1851 judgment is about the apparition, not about them.[13] [12]
